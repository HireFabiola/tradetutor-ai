# Understanding Risk/Reward Ratios

## What is a ratio?

A ratio is simply a comparison between two quantities. Imagine a classroom where the ratio of boys to girls is 2 to 3 — that means for every 2 boys in the room, there are 3 girls. Ratios show up everywhere once you start looking for them, and trading uses the same basic idea.

## What is a risk/reward ratio?

A risk/reward ratio compares the money you're risking on a trade to the money you could potentially earn from it. If a trade has a risk/reward ratio of 2 to 3, that means for every $2 you're risking, you stand to potentially earn $3. The bigger the potential reward compared to the risk, the more favorable the trade looks — at least on paper.

**A quick note on the name itself:** you'll sometimes see traders write this ratio as reward-to-risk instead of risk-to-reward, and it's worth knowing why. "Risk/reward ratio" is the more common *name* for this concept, but it's often actually *calculated* as reward divided by risk — the opposite order the name suggests. That's a well-known point of confusion even among experienced traders, not something wrong with you if it trips you up. This lesson (and this app) calculates it as reward ÷ risk, meaning a higher number always means a larger potential payoff relative to what you're risking.

Calculating it this way — reward divided by risk — has a practical reason behind it, not just convention: it tells you plainly how much you stand to win for every dollar you're putting at risk. A ratio of 2.0 means you stand to earn $2 for every $1 risked; a ratio of 0.5 means you'd only earn 50 cents for every $1 risked. Framed this way, it's easy to see at a glance whether a trade's potential payoff is worth the risk you'd be taking on.

## How to calculate it

To calculate a risk/reward ratio, you need three numbers: your **entry price** (where you get into the trade), your **stop-loss price** (where you'd exit to limit your loss), and your **target price** (where you'd exit to take your profit).

The calculation works like this:
- **Risk** = entry price − stop-loss price
- **Reward** = target price − entry price
- **Risk/reward ratio** = reward ÷ risk

For example, imagine you enter a trade at $40, set a stop-loss at $35, and set a target of $50. Your risk is $5 (the distance from $40 to $35), and your reward is $10 (the distance from $40 to $50). Dividing reward by risk gives a ratio of 2.0 — meaning for every $1 you're risking, you stand to earn $2 if the trade hits its target.

## Why the ratio alone isn't the whole story

It's tempting to think a higher risk/reward ratio always means a better trade, but that's a common misconception. The ratio only tells you what happens *if* the trade works out — it says nothing about *how likely* the trade is to actually hit its target instead of its stop-loss. A trade with an excellent 5-to-1 ratio isn't a good trade if it only succeeds 10% of the time. Risk/reward ratio and win rate work together, not separately, and a trader who only looks at one of the two is missing half the picture.

## A concrete example

Imagine a trader believes a stock currently trading at $40 is likely to rise. They set a stop-loss at $35 in case they're wrong, and a target of $50 if they're right. Using the calculation above, their risk is $5 and their reward is $10, giving a risk/reward ratio of 2.0. Before entering the trade, a disciplined trader would also ask: how confident am I that this trade actually reaches $50 before it falls to $35? A good ratio makes a trade worth considering — it doesn't make it a guaranteed win.
